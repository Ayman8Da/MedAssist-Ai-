1. WHY DIABETES CHANGES THE ASSESSMENT

Adults with diabetes mellitus have a higher risk of severe respiratory
infection and of complicated hospital course. Several mechanisms are
plausible. Hyperglycaemia can impair neutrophil function and opsonisation.
Diabetes is frequently accompanied by cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney
disease, and obesity, all of which reduce physiological reserve.

The practical consequence is that the same respiratory infection can present
later and deteriorate faster in a patient with diabetes. Tachycardia may be
masked by autonomic neuropathy. Fever may be blunted in older adults. Oxygen
saturation may look acceptable while the patient is already tachypnoeic.

2. CLINICAL INFORMATION REQUIRED

A structured assessment should record:
- Vital signs: temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, blood pressure,
  oxygen saturation on room air, and level of consciousness.
- Glucose status: current capillary glucose, recent trend, HbA1c if available,
  and whether the patient is on insulin.
- Renal function: most recent eGFR and creatinine, plus the trend over months.
- Hydration status: fluid intake, urine output, mucosal membranes, orthostatic
  symptoms if safely assessable.
- Respiratory examination: work of breathing, auscultation, ability to speak
  in full sentences.
- Comorbidity: heart failure, chronic kidney disease, prior myocardial
  infarction, immunosuppression, malignancy.
- Medication history: metformin, SGLT2 inhibitors, corticosteroids, diuretics,
  and any recent antibiotic courses.
- Vaccination status for influenza, pneumococcal disease, and SARS-CoV-2.
- Symptom timeline: onset, duration, progression, and sick contacts.

3. RISK FACTORS FOR SEVERE DISEASE

- Age above 65 years.
- Poorly controlled hyperglycaemia at presentation.
- Chronic kidney disease stage 3b or worse.
- Known ischaemic heart disease or heart failure.
- Immunosuppressive therapy or active malignancy.
- Residence in a care facility.
- Prior intensive care admission for respiratory illness.

4. RED FLAGS REQUIRING URGENT IN-PERSON ASSESSMENT

- Oxygen saturation below 92 percent on room air at sea level, or a fall of
  three or more percentage points from the patient's baseline.
- Respiratory rate above 24 breaths per minute.
- Systolic blood pressure below 90 mmHg.
- New confusion or reduced level of consciousness.
- Capillary glucose above 250 mg/dL with symptoms of dehydration or
  ketosis, or any glucose below 70 mg/dL.
- Inability to maintain oral intake.
- Rapidly progressive dyspnoea or cyanosis.

The presence of any red flag means the patient should not be managed through
an asynchronous chat interface. Immediate in-person clinical assessment is
required.

5. METABOLIC INTERACTIONS TO ANTICIPATE

Acute infection commonly worsens glycaemic control. Clinicians frequently need
to review insulin doses and to consider withholding metformin and SGLT2
inhibitors during acute illness, dehydration, or hypoxia, in line with local
protocols. SGLT2 inhibitors carry a risk of euglycaemic ketoacidosis during
acute illness, which is easy to miss when glucose is only mildly elevated.

6. INFORMATION THAT IS OFTEN MISSING

- Exact symptom duration and trajectory.
- Whether the patient has measured temperature at home.
- Recent antibiotic use, which changes the expected microbiology.
- Baseline oxygen saturation and baseline functional status.
- Pregnancy status where relevant.
- Whether the patient lives alone and can be reassessed.

7. UNCERTAINTY NOTE

A decision-support system cannot exclude pneumonia, sepsis, or cardiac
decompensation from text alone. Imaging, laboratory tests, and a physical
examination are required. Recommendations here are general principles and must
be adapted by a qualified clinician to the individual patient.
