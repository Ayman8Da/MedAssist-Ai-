TITLE: Antimicrobial Stewardship Principles for Respiratory Infections
SOURCE LABEL: MedAssist Synthetic Library
VERSION: 1.0
PUBLICATION DATE: 2026-03-08
CATEGORY: infectious_diseases

1. CORE PRINCIPLE

Antibiotics treat bacterial infection. Most acute cough and upper respiratory
illnesses in previously healthy adults are viral and do not benefit from
antibiotics. Prescribing antibiotics for viral illness exposes the patient to
adverse effects and contributes to resistance without clinical benefit.

2. WHEN ANTIBIOTICS ARE MORE LIKELY TO BE APPROPRIATE

- Clinical features suggesting bacterial pneumonia, supported by imaging where
  available.
- Sepsis or haemodynamic instability.
- Exacerbation of COPD with increased sputum purulence and breathlessness.
- Confirmed streptococcal pharyngitis.
- Immunosuppression with a compatible clinical picture.

The decision should be based on the whole clinical picture, not on sputum
colour alone. Sputum colour is a poor discriminator between viral and bacterial
infection.

3. INFORMATION REQUIRED BEFORE PRESCRIBING

- Objective vital signs and oxygen saturation.
- Comorbidities, especially chronic lung disease and immunosuppression.
- Recent antibiotic exposure within the last three months.
- Known colonisation with resistant organisms.
- Allergy history, including the nature of the previous reaction.
- Renal and hepatic function, which affect drug selection and dose.
- Local resistance patterns and current stewardship guidance.
- Whether the patient can be reassessed within 48 hours.

4. DE-ESCALATION AND REVIEW

- Review intravenous therapy at 48 to 72 hours and switch to oral treatment
  when the patient is stable and absorbing enteral medication.
- Stop antibiotics when bacterial infection is excluded.
- Narrow the spectrum when culture results permit.
- Document the indication, the intended duration, and the review date.

5. COMMON STEWARDSHIP PITFALLS

- Continuing antibiotics because the patient "looks unwell" without a defined
  indication.
- Treating colonisation rather than infection.
- Using broad-spectrum agents when narrow-spectrum agents are adequate.
- Failing to review therapy after negative cultures.
- Omitting a stop date.

6. SAFETY CONSIDERATIONS

Antibiotic allergy labels are often inaccurate. A documented allergy should be
characterised by the type and timing of the reaction, because incorrect labels
drive use of broader-spectrum agents. However, a history of anaphylaxis or
severe cutaneous adverse reaction requires genuine avoidance and specialist
input.

7. MISSING INFORMATION IN SHORT VIGNETTES

- Culture and sensitivity results.
- Prior microbiology from the same patient.
- Exact antibiotic doses already given.
- Whether the patient is improving or deteriorating.
- Local formulary and resistance data.

8. LIMITATION

This document describes general stewardship principles for software testing.
It does not provide dosing, does not replace local policy, and must not be used
to make prescribing decisions for a real patient.
